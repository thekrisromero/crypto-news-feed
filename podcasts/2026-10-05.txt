Welcome to your weekly Crypto Custody and Treasury briefing for the week of October fifth, twenty twenty-six. This one covers September twenty-eighth through today. It was a busy week for anyone who holds assets for other people. The S E C put out a custody proposal, community banks sued the O C C, and an Ethereum layer two announced it's closing. Let's start with the three things that matter most, then go section by section.

Takeaway number one. The S E C wants more kinds of custodians, and the banks are suing to keep the list short. On October first, the S E C proposed rules that would let registered investment advisers and funds use state chartered trust companies as custodians, and in some cases hold crypto themselves. Two days later, the Independent Community Bankers of America sued the O C C to stop it from granting national trust charters to crypto firms. So the question of who counts as a qualified custodian is being argued in two places at once, pulling in opposite directions. Here's the so what. Whichever side wins, clients are going to ask us to prove our controls instead of pointing at a charter. We should map the S E C's self custody conditions against our policy engine and audit trail now, while the comment window is open.

Takeaway number two. Stablecoin issuers are now paying for distribution. Stripe's Bridge launched a stablecoin called O U S D that shares its economics with the platforms that distribute it. Visa said volume on its stablecoin linked cards grew almost two hundred percent year over year. And Lloyds settled real payment obligations with Visa in U S D C. The so what: if we hold client stablecoin balances, we are a distribution channel. We should find out what that's worth, and what we'd have to disclose to clients if we took it.

Takeaway number three. This week's losses came from the plumbing around the keys, and one chain simply closed. MetaMask had validator rewards stolen from its staking operation. NEAR Intents had a bug in its deposit and withdrawal logic. Arbitrum paused a feature as an emergency measure. And Blast, which was once a two billion dollar layer two, is shutting down with an October twenty-sixth deadline for normal withdrawals. None of these were classic private key thefts. The so what: we need two runbooks we don't have yet. One for retiring a chain, and one for a chain that declares an emergency.

Now the sections. First up, custody and wallet infrastructure.

The headline is the S E C proposal. Under the draft, advisers and funds could use state trust companies as custodians, and could self custody under certain conditions. Comments are due sixty days after it's published in the Federal Register. The Block, CoinDesk and Decrypt all covered it. For us, the interesting part is the self custody conditions. If an adviser can hold crypto directly, the S E C is going to want evidence of controls. That means approval policies, audit logs, segregation reporting. Some of what we've treated as nice to have could become required. We should read those conditions line by line against our product, and decide whether we want to file a comment.

Next, Anchorage Digital. CoinTelegraph reports the four point two billion dollar crypto bank is cutting about seventeen percent of its staff. That's happening while Anchorage pushes into stablecoin issuance and takes a hundred million dollar investment from Tether. A direct competitor is narrowing its focus. I'd expect institutional prospects to start asking harder questions about vendor runway, so we should have our continuity and exit plan documentation ready for R F P responses.

Third, MetaMask. Protos reports that MetaMask disclosed a security incident in its staking operations where validator rewards were stolen. It then unstaked about one point four billion dollars of E T H. MetaMask said user wallets faced no immediate threat. The lesson here is that fee recipient and withdrawal addresses are their own attack surface, separate from client keys. We should check that changing those on a staked position goes through the same approval policy as an outbound transfer. If it doesn't, that's a gap.

Fourth, the Bitget hack. Last week we talked about how Bitget lost three hundred eighty-seven million dollars through spoofed internal transfers. This week Chainalysis tied it to North Korea. The firm says the September twenty-fourth breach pushed North Korea linked thefts in twenty twenty-six past one billion dollars. It used in-house A I tools to trace the funds across four chains, and stolen X R P was swapped through THORChain. The counterparty is now a sanctioned state actor. We should confirm our deposit screening flags addresses tied to the Bitget hack, including funds that passed through THORChain.

Last in this section, NEAR Intents. On October first, a deposit and withdrawal exploit drained three point eight million dollars and disrupted deposits and withdrawals on eleven networks. The team patched it quickly, said it had identified the attacker, and gave them forty-eight hours. The money came back in full, about thirty-four and a half B T C. That's a good outcome, but the bigger point is that intent and solver networks are turning into the place where withdrawals actually settle. If we route anything through one, we need outage monitoring and a fallback rail.

On to stablecoins and payments.

Circle filed with the European Commission as part of the MiCA review. It wants the E U to replace mandatory bank deposit minimums with more flexible liquidity rules, keep cross border co-issuance, and create a recognition route for stablecoins issued outside Europe. Central banks are pushing for stronger safeguards instead. How this lands decides whether U S D C issued outside the E U stays usable for our European clients. The practical takeaway: keep stablecoin eligibility configurable by issuer and jurisdiction. Don't hardcode it.

Bridge, the stablecoin company Stripe owns, launched O U S D. It's live on four chains, and it pays the platforms that distribute it. That's a business model change for issuers, and it's a revenue question for us. Since we hold client stablecoin balances, we might qualify as a distributor. It's worth a call with Bridge to learn the terms, and a conversation with legal about what client disclosure that would require.

Visa said business programs drive about seventeen percent of the volume on its stablecoin linked cards, and total volume grew nearly two hundred percent year over year in its fiscal second quarter. Corporate spend from stablecoin balances is real now. Treasury clients will ask for cards funded directly from custody balances, so a card issuer integration should go on the discovery list.

Lloyds and Visa ran a seven day live cross border pilot in which Lloyds settled seven hundred fifty thousand dollars of payment obligations in U S D C. A U K clearing bank settling real obligations in a stablecoin is exactly the use case our treasury product is built for. Whatever reporting they needed, things like F X reference rates, settlement confirmations and general ledger reconciliation, belongs in our requirements too.

And Fiserv launched something called Roughrider coin on Solana. VersaBank issues it, and it's bank only. Fiserv says it serves a North Dakota network of more than ninety banks and credit unions. Permissioned tokens on public chains are a new asset type for us. Our allowlist model needs to handle a token that only verified bank wallets can hold or receive.

Next, treasury, real world assets and corporate adoption.

A joint venture between O K X and I C E, the parent company of the New York Stock Exchange, filed to launch round the clock trading in tokenized U S stocks. It wants to offer tokenized shares in more than sixty U S listed companies under the S E C's new innovation exemption. Robinhood's crypto chief said that same exemption still limits how fast stock tokens can come to the U S, so this isn't a done deal. But if tokenized equities trade twenty-four seven, it breaks our assumption that markets close. We should list everything that depends on a close. Valuation snapshots, corporate actions, reporting cut offs.

In Brazil, the central securities depository C S D B R is putting B T G Pactual fund share records on the public X R P Ledger, working with Ripple. C S D B R's own records stay the legal source of truth. The token is a mirror. When that's the setup, a custodian has to show both records and reconcile them. A field on each tokenized asset that says where the legal record lives, plus a reconciliation status, would cover it.

And Strategy. Protos reports that after ten weeks of buybacks, Strategy has used sixty-four percent of its two billion dollar S T R C rescue fund and has less than seven hundred twenty-four million left. Separately, crypto treasury companies are having a hard time holding on to their valuation premiums. Treasury companies under pressure may sell or pledge B T C. For that segment, collateral and fast unwind workflows will matter more than long term cold storage.

Now regulation and policy.

We touched on this at the top. The I C B A sued the O C C. The complaint targets the conditional charter approval for a company called Protego, and asks the court to stop the O C C from granting national trust charters under its current rule and guidance. The banking group calls the charters a side door into the banking system. Several of our competitors are planning around a national trust charter. If this suit succeeds, state trust charters, which the S E C proposal accepts, become more valuable. We should track which partners depend on O C C charters.

BitGo's C E O Mike Belshe told The Block that the failure of the Clarity Act left systemic risk in firms that run exchange, brokerage and custody together. He said it could be worse than Lehman. Bitwise's Matt Hougan sees it the other way: he says Clarity's death gave crypto faster wins at the S E C. For us, independence from trading venues is a real selling point, but only with proof. Segregation, no rehypothecation, a separate legal entity. Those belong in the sales deck.

Illinois agreed to delay its crypto tax by six months, though that still needs court approval. The levy would charge customers on the value of their assets, not their gains, and the draft rules cover paid custody, transfers and stablecoin settlement. A tax on value held in paid custody would hit our fee lines for Illinois clients. We should ask legal whether we'd be the party collecting it, and check whether billing can carry a tax line by jurisdiction.

And on sanctions. The U S designated Russia's A 7 network as a transnational criminal organization. Treasury's proposed rule would cut A 7's front companies off from U S finance, and FinCEN proposed banning sub agent payments. FinCEN says A 7 sub agents processed more than seventeen billion dollars in dollar transactions between January twenty twenty-five and June twenty twenty-six, and A 7's ruble token moved one hundred seventy-nine billion. Japan also added Garantex to its sanctions list. We should make sure our screening lists cover A 7 linked entities and its ruble token, and confirm our vendor has the Japan Garantex listing.

Last section, smart contracts and infrastructure.

Blast is shutting down its Ethereum layer two because costs are higher than revenue. Users have until October twenty-sixth to withdraw through the normal interface. After that, withdrawals still work, but only by calling the contracts directly. About sixty-three and a half million dollars is still sitting in the bridge. If any client holds assets on Blast, we should reach out before the twenty-sixth. Then we should write the chain retirement runbook, covering client notice, forced withdrawal and delisting, so the next one is routine.

Arbitrum paused new Stylus activations as an emergency security measure. Existing Stylus contracts keep running. A new guard in its BoLD dispute system could delay unconfirmed withdrawals if contradictory proofs are accepted. The point is that layer two withdrawal timing can change in the middle of an incident. Our Arbitrum withdrawal estimates should come from live chain status, and ops should get an alert when a chain declares an emergency.

Finally, OpenZeppelin and the T REX Network rebuilt ONCHAINID as a smart account. Version three lets one identity hold regulated tokens, make transactions and handle recovery. It requires new deployments, so existing version two identities can't be upgraded in place. Plenty of tokenized funds use the T REX standard, also known as E R C 3 6 4 3. If we support those tokens, we need a migration plan for clients on version two identities, and we should check how version three recovery interacts with our own key recovery flow.
