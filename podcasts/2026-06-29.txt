Now let's go theme by theme.

First, Custody and Wallet Infrastructure.

The headline here is BitGo. Six months after its I P O, the custodian is cutting nearly fifteen percent of its staff and refocusing on stablecoins, settlement, and A I infrastructure. This is our most direct competitor signaling that pure custody margins are thin and that stablecoin settlement is where the growth is. That validates pushing settlement and stablecoin features up our own roadmap, and it may open a window to recruit displaced custody and key-management talent.

Next, SecondFi. An exploit drained about two point four million dollars in A D A, the Cardano token, from three hundred and seventy-four addresses over three days, through a flaw in SecondFi's wallet-generation software. The firm says it aims to return funds within two weeks. A wallet-generation and entropy flaw is exactly the failure mode our key-ceremony and M P C, that's multi-party computation, design has to rule out. This is a good prompt for a security review of our address-derivation and entropy sourcing, plus a documented incident-recovery service-level agreement we can actually market.

Related to that, the mystery around the so-called white-hat hacker moving about eighteen and a half million dollars in linked funds deepened this week, with conflicting claims from Cardano's founder about whether the actor is genuinely known. The lesson for custodians: we need a pre-defined, legally-vetted playbook for white-hat fund returns and counterparty verification. Ambiguity here is reputational risk.

Second theme, Stablecoins and Payments.

Ripple launched R L U S D in Japan through S B I, becoming the first foreign-issued stablecoin classified under Japan's revised Payment Services Act, with Circle and Nomura moving on the same market. Jurisdiction-specific approvals like this mean our treasury clients will increasingly want to hold and settle in regional regulated coins. We should scope R L U S D support and build a framework for onboarding new jurisdiction-approved stablecoins quickly.

Then MoneyGram. Its chief executive detailed rolling out M G U S D to sixty million users, backed by a Kraken partnership, a validator seat on the Tempo network, and more than two billion dollars in stablecoin settlements already running. A payments incumbent issuing its own coin and running a validator is exactly the enterprise treasury customer we want. It signals demand for custody and settlement of branded, partner stablecoins, which is a potential business-to-business-to-consumer wedge for us.

On the macro side, the Bank for International Settlements, the B I S, warned that stablecoins risk fragmenting the global financial system and fall short of the requirements for sound money, urging policymakers to accelerate tokenized central-bank and commercial-bank money instead. That stance foreshadows tighter reserve and redemption rules, and a possible tokenized-deposit competitor. We should design custody to be issuer-agnostic and ready to support tokenized bank money, rather than betting the roadmap on any single stablecoin model.

And one data point worth sitting with: the stablecoin founder map does not match the volume map. Emerging markets drive most real-world stablecoin usage, yet founders and venture funding stay concentrated in the U S and Europe. Real demand is in Latin America, Africa, and Southeast Asia. If our custody and treasury product targets only U S and European institutions, we miss where the volume actually lives.

Third theme, Treasury, Real-World Assets, and Corporate Adoption.

The big one is Securitize. The tokenization platform behind BlackRock's BUIDL fund is going public on the New York Stock Exchange on July second, under the ticker S E C Z, after a SPAC merger that raised more than four hundred million dollars, including an oversubscribed two hundred and twenty-five million dollar private placement. A public tokenization leader raises the bar on real-world-asset custody integrations. Our treasury product should be able to custody and report on tokenized funds like BUIDL, so we need to confirm we have, or can build, the connectors and the net-asset-value and reporting hooks.

Ondo Finance enabled around-the-clock, twenty-four-seven minting and redemption for tokenized U S stocks and exchange-traded funds on Ethereum and B N B Chain, removing the weekday-only constraint tied to U S market hours. That means our custody and reconciliation systems must handle off-hours settlement and intraday net-asset-value. Design our treasury reporting for continuous asset flows, not just market-hours flows.

On the data front, distributed tokenized real-world-asset value stalled at roughly thirty-one and a half billion dollars, slipping about one point four percent over the month. That's the first monthly decline of its institutional-led run, even as the number of holders climbed fourteen percent, led by tokenized stocks. Value flat but holders up means broadening retail access at smaller ticket sizes. Our real-world-asset custody pricing and user experience should anticipate many smaller positions, not just a few whale allocations.

We also saw StablecoinX begin trading on the Nasdaq under the ticker U S D E, becoming the first publicly listed company holding ENA tokens, with about three billion of them, and building infrastructure for the Ethena ecosystem. Single-asset public treasury vehicles keep proliferating beyond Bitcoin. Each one is a potential custody client with concentrated-asset, audit-grade reporting needs, and concentration risk we can help them monitor.

And the cautionary tale: Strategy's enterprise m N A V dropped below one for the first time, putting its debt, preferred stock, and equity above the value of its eight hundred and forty-seven thousand Bitcoin treasury, with around thirteen billion dollars in unrealized losses, and its STRC preferred shares trading about twenty-five percent below par. The flagship corporate-treasury model is under visible strain. Treasury clients will want real-time m N A V, coverage, and liquidation-risk dashboards. That's a strong case for adding balance-sheet risk monitoring to our platform.

Fourth theme, Regulation and Policy.

We covered the headline already, but to expand: with Binance winding down in the European Union, Coinbase and O K X are courting its roughly four hundred and fifty million users, and a massive pool of E U assets is now in motion looking for licensed homes. If we hold, or partner for, MiCA-aligned custody, this is a concrete acquisition moment, and we should align sales and partnerships to those migrating institutional flows.

Reinforcing the point, Spain's regulator ruled out any extension for non-compliant firms, saying there will be no exceptions or extensions to the MiCA deadline, naming Binance specifically. National regulators are enforcing hard, not granting grace periods. Our E U go-to-market, and any partner exchanges, must assume strict enforcement.

In the United States, the Securities and Exchange Commission, the S E C, and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission, the C F T C, are jointly seeking public input on unified portfolio-margin rules across securities and derivatives, covering cross-margining, collateral, and risk management. Cross-margining across asset classes raises demand for unified, real-time collateral custody and reporting. That's a potential feature lane for us, and it may be worth submitting a comment to help shape custodian-friendly rules.

And European lawmakers issued a nonbinding report urging assessment of DeFi, staking, and N F T regulation, while warning against fragmented national interpretations of MiCA. Staking custody is squarely in the European Union's sights. If we offer, or plan, staking-as-custody, we need to track this closely, because the rules could affect how we structure delegated staking and reporting for E U clients.

Fifth theme, Smart Contracts and Infrastructure.

The standout security story is Polymarket. Hackers infiltrated its website through a compromised third-party vendor, draining about three point one million dollars across eleven wallets, with funds traced to Ethereum, and Polymarket has promised refunds. Notice the chain itself was not hacked; the front-end vendor was. That's a direct prompt to audit our own web and third-party script supply chain, things like content-security policy, sub-resource integrity, and vendor review, and to message clients on how our signing flow resists front-end tampering.

On reliability, Base, the layer-two network, said a sequencer bug caused back-to-back mainnet outages, halting block production twice in under twenty-four hours, blamed on a race condition after a system reset. Single-sequencer layer-two downtime can freeze the deposits and withdrawals we custody. We need graceful degradation and clear client communications for chain halts, and we should weight layer-two reliability when we decide which networks to support.

In a sign of the times, the pioneering zero-knowledge rollup Loopring is closing its decentralized exchange, citing lack of adoption, with the team pointing to no virtual machine, no composability, and no real-world payment use cases. The lesson: infrastructure without real payment or composability use cases does not survive. When we choose which chains and rollups to support for custody, we should weight live payment and real-world-asset traction over technical novelty.

And finally, the Linux Foundation and a coalition of nineteen organizations, including major A I labs and Wall Street banks, launched a group called Akrites to defend open source against A I-powered attacks. Our custody stack rides on open-source cryptographic libraries that are now A I-attack targets. It's worth tracking Akrites and tightening our dependency-scanning and software-bill-of-materials practices for our signing and key-management code.
