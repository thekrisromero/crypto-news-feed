Welcome to your weekly Crypto Custody and Treasury briefing for the week of August twenty fourth, twenty twenty six. This edition covers the seven days from August seventeenth through August twenty fourth. One hundred fifty seven stories came through the feed this week, and I have picked twenty one that matter for a custody and treasury product team.

Let me start with the three things that matter most.

First. The Treasury Department proposed rules for how stablecoin licensing works under the GENIUS Act, and the notable part is where the obligation lands. The proposal would put a due diligence duty on the venues that list stablecoins, extending the obligation past the issuers themselves. So what does that mean for us. It means our per asset onboarding review stops being an internal risk memo and starts being a regulatory artifact we may have to produce on demand. If that duty reaches platforms like ours, we need a standing, versioned issuer file for every stablecoin we support, and we want that scoped before the comment period closes rather than after.

Second. Know your agent showed up as real custody product this week, not as a conference panel topic. Anchorage Digital launched an agentic banking platform with agent level identity checks, and their chief executive argued directly that A I agents need their own bank accounts. In parallel, CoinDesk ran a piece arguing that the next billion crypto users may be software agents paying each other in stablecoins, Binance opened trading to A I agents with user set controls, and Ramp added agent payments. So what. Our permission model quietly assumes a human principal sits behind every approval. That assumption now needs a design review, because a federally chartered competitor has already staked a claim on non human account holders.

Third. Three genuinely different failure classes hit in a single week. Seed generation at Coldcard. Bridge mint authority at The Sandbox. And vault governance at Term Finance. So what. Not one of those was a stolen private key. Our control set is heavy on protecting keys and light on verifying provenance and monitoring authority. This is a good week to make the roadmap argument for entropy source attestation and mint authority alerting, because the evidence is sitting right there.

Now let me walk through the sections.

Starting with custody and wallet infrastructure.

Coldcard shipped a firmware fix following a bitcoin exploit reported at one hundred thirty million dollars by Decrypt and one hundred fourteen million by CoinDesk. The new firmware requires users to contribute their own randomness when generating a wallet seed, and patches further issues found during a three week review. So what for us. This is an entropy quality failure, not a key theft failure, and our diligence questionnaire for hardware signers does not ask how seeds are generated or whether the random number generator is attestable. Add an entropy provenance question to vendor review, and check whether any customer held signing devices in our supported set share the affected generation path.

Anchorage Digital built an agentic banking platform with what they call a know your agent setup, designed to support machine initiated transactions. So what. Our roles and approvals model has no principal type for a service identity carrying its own spend limits and revocation path. Retrofitting that into the policy engine is a quarter of work, not a sprint, so the design document should start now rather than when the first client asks.

OpenZeppelin published a useful piece on identity and access control for institutional participation on public blockchains. Their framing is that to a protocol, every address is just a string with a balance. There is no native field for verified investor or sanctioned entity, so institutions working with tokenized assets are building that layer themselves in contract logic. So what. If tokenized assets increasingly enforce allowlists at the contract level, a custody wallet that is not on the allowlist cannot receive the asset at all. We should be able to register client wallet addresses with issuer identity registries during onboarding, and surface allowlist status in the asset support matrix.

Arbitrum activated a release called Elara that gives optional compliance screening on dedicated chains, with the chain owner rather than the Arbitrum D A O controlling it. So what. Compliance filtered application chains are now a live deployment target for bank clients, which turns which chain an asset sits on into a control question rather than an integration detail. Our chain support documentation should record screening policy per network so sales can answer it without escalating.

T R M Labs reported that A I use in crypto crime rose forty percent over the past year, with attackers using A I to find overlooked vulnerabilities and to infiltrate I T service firms. So what. The I T vendor infiltration angle touches us directly. That is a supply chain and social engineering path into approval workflows, not a cryptography problem. Push for out of band verification on any change to approver details.

Moving to stablecoins and payments.

I covered the GENIUS Act proposal in the takeaways, but I want to underline that it was the highest signal item of the week. We are not an exchange, but a platform that makes a stablecoin available to clients sits close enough to the described activity that counsel should read it and tell us where we land.

Deel took its D L U S D stablecoin wallet to more than eighty countries. The dollar balance is issued through Stripe's Bridge and settles on Tempo, live across Latin America, Africa, the Middle East and Asia Pacific. The United States, United Kingdom, European Union and Australia remain excluded. So what. This is a branded, single purpose dollar token settling on a rail we may not support. Treasury clients paying international contractors will eventually ask us to hold and reconcile balances like this. The product question is whether we support long tail issuer tokens generically or maintain a curated list, and that decision is easier before the first customer request than during it.

Crypto linked card spending passed one billion dollars, with stablecoins moving into ordinary purchases. So what. Card spend means many small debits against a corporate balance, a very different traffic shape from the large scheduled transfers our approval flows are tuned for. To sit behind a corporate spend program we need policy tiers that auto approve below a threshold, plus reporting that rolls thousands of small outflows into something a controller can reconcile.

CoinDesk argued that A I agents paying in stablecoins are the next wave of users. So what. This pairs with the Anchorage item as the same signal from two directions. The concrete near term ask is scoped A P I credentials with their own velocity limits, plus an audit trail recording which agent acted under which human's delegated authority. Tractable, and the entry point into the larger agentic story.

Circle is targeting September sixteenth for the Arc public mainnet launch. Official materials name Circle plus eleven founding validators, while Arc documentation describes roughly twenty at launch. So what. A hard date three weeks out on a chain built by the U S D C issuer, so client demand for Arc support is a question of when rather than whether. Get an engineering estimate into the next planning cycle, and note the validator count discrepancy as a decentralization question for our chain risk assessment.

Next, treasury, real world assets and corporate adoption.

H S B C and Standard Chartered completed the first live tokenized deposit transfer on Swift's blockchain based ledger. The context matters. Most tokenized deposits today run on single bank chains, so they only work between customers of that same bank. Swift's ledger matched and netted obligations recorded on the two banks' separate platforms, although final settlement still ran through existing systems. So what. Tokenized bank deposits are the most credible competitor to stablecoins for corporate treasury, and Swift interoperability removes their biggest limitation. A treasury dashboard that only shows public chain assets will look incomplete to a chief financial officer holding both. Worth a discovery conversation about whether we aggregate tokenized deposit positions read only, before someone asks.

South Korea's Shinhan bank partnered with the Solana Foundation, Etherfuse and Orca on a proof of concept for a Korean won denominated tokenized fund. So what. Non dollar tokenized money market instruments are now coming from major banks, and they stress our asset model specifically. These need multi currency net asset value, yield accrual and redemption windows, not simple token balances. If our data model treats every holding as a spot token, tokenized funds will not report correctly.

On tokenized equities, two items paired well. Robinhood's Vlad Tenev published a case for United States equity tokenization, five days after the S E C shelved its innovation exemption for a second time. Separately, the chief executive of Fairmint warned that tokenized stocks risk repeating Wall Street's nineteen sixties paper crisis if record keeping does not keep pace. So what. The paper crisis framing is the useful part. If tokenized equities arrive with ambiguous beneficial ownership records, custody providers who can produce a clean, auditable holder record become the fix rather than another layer of the problem. That is a positioning argument for our reporting and attestation work.

Digital Asset and the Paul Ryan foundation announced a pilot on the Canton Network to distribute state administered benefits across three United States states beginning in twenty twenty seven. So what. Public sector disbursement on a permissioned chain points at a segment with real custody requirements and procurement cycles that begin years ahead. Not a twenty twenty six revenue line, but if government treasury sits anywhere on our long term map, Canton support belongs in the strategy document.

On to regulation and policy.

The S E C proposed a framework called Regulation Crypto Assets. It pairs two registration exemptions with a conditional safe harbor that would take the investment contract label off a token, and it preempts state securities law. All three sitting commissioners backed it in a vote held outside a public meeting, and the exemption ceiling is seventy five million dollars. So what. A safe harbor that strips the investment contract label changes which assets we can list without running a securities analysis on each one. If it lands roughly as proposed, our listing pipeline gets faster and the bottleneck moves from legal review to engineering integration. Ask counsel how the safe harbor conditions map against our current listing checklist.

The C F T C chair, Michael Selig, said the agency will move forward on crypto regulation if the CLARITY Act fails, and that he has directed staff to explore developer protections. So what. This reduces the odds our compliance roadmap stalls waiting on legislation, and raises the odds of two overlapping regimes to satisfy. Keep the compliance backlog structured around specific obligations rather than around which bill passes.

In Europe, Brussels is reviewing whether crypto lending should fall under M i C A, and DeFi lending vaults make it hard to identify who the regulated party even is. So what. Any yield or staking feature we offer European Union clients could get pulled into a lending definition, and how we present it in the interface is part of how that gets judged. If yield is on the roadmap for our European entities, get the product framing reviewed before build.

And in Japan, Nomura backed Laser Digital won the country's first crypto exchange approval in four years, registering to provide domestic liquidity ahead of institutional trading. So what. Japan reopening its licensing pipeline with a bank affiliated firm first through the door is a signal about sequencing if Asia Pacific expansion is on our map, and bank affiliated venues tend to need a qualified custodian. Treat it as a partnership lead, not just a market entry datapoint.

Finally, smart contracts and infrastructure.

The Sandbox said it contained a bridge exploit that minted unbacked S A N D tokens on Base and B S C. Upbit and Bithumb froze S A N D transfers under South Korea's user protection law, and Upbit halted deposits and withdrawals on Ethereum, which is the chain the studio says was not affected. So what. There are two lessons here. First, a client can be holding an unbacked bridged token while our balance display shows it as perfectly fine, so supply invariant monitoring on bridged assets is a real gap in our controls. Second, the exchanges froze the unaffected chain as well, which means our incident runbook should assume conservative blanket freezes and should tell clients what is actually withdrawable during an event.

MANTRA halted its chain and pointed at the Cosmos E V M module. The Dubai licensed real world asset Layer One has been frozen since Thursday evening. MANTRA says the incident touched two wallets it controls and that no user funds were taken, and it has not said whether anything left those wallets. So what. A licensed real world asset chain going dark is precisely the tail risk clients raise when we pitch tokenized assets, and a shared Cosmos E V M module means the blast radius may extend past one chain. Our chain risk tiering should account for shared dependency correlation, and we need a defined client facing status for assets sitting on a halted network. Balance shown, transfers unavailable, duration indefinite is not currently a state our interface can express.

Term Finance lost an estimated eight and a half million dollars in a vault governance exploit, and permanently closed its Meta Vaults after the attack reportedly removed nearly all of their Ethereum deposits. So what. Governance was the attack surface, not the contract math, which is exactly the failure mode that audits tend to miss. Any protocol we expose through a yield or DeFi feature needs governance parameter review in the integration checklist. Who can change vault configuration, over what timelock, and do we alert clients when those parameters move.

And Solana cut its mainnet slot time to three hundred fifty milliseconds, a first step toward a two hundred millisecond goal. Confirmations get faster, though the change is not designed to raise overall network throughput. So what. Our Solana confirmation depth is very likely specified in slots, which means a shorter slot gives us less wall clock finality for the same setting. It is worth an engineering check that our confirmation policy is time based, or was re derived after this change, and the same review applies to any indexer that assumes fixed slot timing.
