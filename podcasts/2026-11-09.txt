Welcome to your weekly Crypto Custody and Treasury briefing for the week of November ninth, twenty twenty-six. This one covers roughly November second through the ninth.

It was a heavy week. The fallout from the Binance hack kept spreading, Treasury cut a major exchange out of the US financial system, and another crypto company moved closer to a federal bank charter. Let's start with the three things that matter most, and then go section by section.

Takeaway number one. The Binance hack was not a smash and grab. The Security Alliance, known as SEAL, says the attackers had about eight weeks of access inside Binance before they moved six point three billion dollars. And in the same week we saw a run of attacks on the plumbing around wallets rather than the keys themselves. Keplr's Google and Apple login was abused. Threshold paused its t B T C bridge because of a Linux kernel bug. Mercado Bitcoin's deposit addresses were drained. And Gnosis Pay leaked card data. So what for us? Buyers are going to ask how we would notice someone living inside our infrastructure for two months. We need a clean answer on dwell time detection, anomaly alerts on signing activity, and how fast we can patch the hosts our signers run on.

Takeaway number two. Fin Sen, the Treasury's financial crimes unit, ordered US financial institutions to stop all transfers to and from the exchange MEXC. And O K X is already holding U S D T deposits that come from MEXC, with its C E O saying they treat the order much like sanctions. So what? US clients can't send to or receive from MEXC, starting now. We should confirm that our screening vendor tags MEXC deposit and hot wallet addresses, decide what operations does with inbound funds that trace back to MEXC, and update the client notice.

Takeaway number three. Bridge, Stripe's stablecoin company, got approval from the O C C on its path to a federal stablecoin trust bank. That's the second crypto native trust charter in two weeks, after Ripple. Bank chartered issuers that also do custody are turning into direct competitors. There was some good news on the regulatory side too. Treasury and the Federal Reserve withdrew an old proposal that would have dropped the travel rule threshold for cross border transfers from three thousand dollars to two hundred and fifty. So there's no near term rework needed on our Travel Rule thresholds.

Now let's go through the sections, starting with custody and wallet infrastructure.

First, more detail on the SEAL findings. The Security Alliance describes a multi stage intrusion with about eight weeks of access before any funds moved, and says it has seen the same pattern behind other big heists, including the three hundred and eighty-eight million dollar Bitget hack. Protos also reports that the breach pushed US officials to have banks check their own systems. The point here is that the keys weren't the weak link. The environment around them was. I'd add a dwell time scenario to our threat model: an attacker with admin access to signing infrastructure for weeks. Which alerts fire? Who sees them? And can the policy engine block a transfer even when the person starting it looks legitimate?

Second, Binance reopened withdrawals, and users pulled a net three point six four billion dollars in the first few hours. Binance also published a proof of reserves report covering ten major tokens. Elliptic says the attackers have swapped stolen tokens for about a hundred and twenty-four million dollars in bitcoin, that two point eight five billion dollars is still frozen, and that very little has actually been cashed out. The F B I has attributed the attack to North Korea. Two lessons. Clients move money fast when trust breaks, three and a half billion dollars in hours. And roughly three billion dollars is still waiting to be laundered, so some of it will try to pass through custodians. Our address screening needs to pick up the Binance hack attribution sets as soon as they're published.

Third, Keplr. The Cosmos wallet paused Google and Apple login after users reported unauthorized transactions. Seed phrase and hardware wallet accounts look unaffected. Web3Auth, which runs the social login key infrastructure, says it frontran the attackers by sweeping five point six million dollars out of exposed wallets into recovery addresses. But its messages also mention an original attacker, so some funds were probably stolen. What I take from this: social login wallets trade seed phrase risk for identity provider and vendor risk. And notice that Web3Auth was able to move user funds at all. If we ever offer lighter weight wallets for end users or treasury sub accounts, the auth vendor's key share design and its emergency powers have to be part of the review.

Fourth, the t B T C bridge. Threshold halted it on October thirtieth while operators patched a critical Linux kernel bug that allowed code execution on unpatched servers. The bridge is running onchain again, but the app is still paused, and Aave's roughly one hundred and twenty million dollar t B T C reserve stays frozen until more operators reconnect. An operating system bug froze a lending reserve for ten days. That's the question to ask infrastructure: how long does it take us to patch a critical kernel C V E on signer and node hosts, and can we rotate hosts without stopping withdrawals?

Fifth, Mercado Bitcoin, Brazil's largest exchange. It said the hack was contained. Fresh forensic work traces forty-eight point six million dollars across eleven chains and shows thousands of customer deposit addresses drained hours after that statement. Two takeaways. The keys behind deposit addresses are a target in their own right and need the same controls as treasury keys. And if we ever have an incident, what we say publicly has to match what the chain will show, because people will check.

On to stablecoins and payments.

The Bridge story first. The O C C's conditional order requires forty-five million dollars in minimum capital and preopening checks. The Defiant points out that a bank charter doesn't replace issuer approval under the GENIUS Act, so Bridge still has another step. Still, a payments giant now has a route to federally chartered custody and issuance. Bridge goes into the competitive matrix next to Ripple and Anchorage, and we should think about where we partner with chartered issuers rather than compete with them head on.

Next, a CoinDesk analysis with a blunt headline: stablecoin growth has stalled, but the payments boom hasn't. The feed only gave us the headline, so take the details with some caution. It does line up with other data, though. Sky's U S D S supply fell to five point five nine billion dollars, and Sky's total value locked dropped fifteen percent in a week. If balances are flat but flows are growing, value moves from holding stablecoins to moving them. That points our roadmap toward payouts, settlement and reconciliation, and away from yield on idle balances.

Circle reported third quarter results. Revenue rose seventeen percent to eight hundred and sixty-five million dollars, and a hundred and eighty-two million of that came from a presale of its ARC token. Interest income on U S D C reserves slipped, and Circle is cutting five percent of staff. With rates squeezing reserve income, Circle is leaning on its Arc chain. Expect it to push Arc settlement hard with institutions, which means we should decide whether Arc belongs on our chain roadmap for next half.

Gnosis Pay, the crypto debit card provider, disclosed that attackers got into the legacy system behind its branded cards on November third. Card numbers, home addresses and linked wallet addresses were exposed. Funds weren't touched, and the enterprise card platform wasn't affected. Tying a wallet address to a home address is a physical safety problem for holders. If we ever add card or spend features, wallet to identity mapping shouldn't live in the card processor, and legacy systems need a firm retirement date.

And Nigeria. The central bank banned algorithmic stablecoins and now requires local licenses and reserves for issuers and other service providers. One more market where the allowed asset list depends on local rules. Our per jurisdiction asset controls need to handle bans by asset type, like algorithmic stablecoins, not just by named token.

Next section: treasury, real world assets, and corporate adoption.

Coinbase now lets eligible US users borrow U S D C against Paxos' tokenized gold, PAXG, through Morpho on Base. It's the first non crypto collateral for Coinbase Borrow. Tokenized real world assets are starting to work as collateral, not just as something you hold. Treasury clients will want to see posted collateral, loan to value and liquidation levels right next to their custody balances, and that's a reporting gap for us today.

In Europe, Amundi launched a tokenized share class of its Core STOXX Europe 600 fund, which manages twenty-two point eight billion euros. It's issued on public Ethereum, with CACEIS acting as transfer agent and tokenization agent. Ledger Insights says it's the first big European equity index fund to do this. For our E U clients, supporting permissioned fund tokens, with allowlists and transfer agent controls, matters more right now than tokenized US stocks, which are still stuck. On that note, Apple and Meta joined the rest of the biggest US tech companies in opting out of the O K X and I C E tokenized stock venue.

Bitcoin treasuries had a rough week. Bitcoin dropped below Strategy's average purchase price of about seventy-five thousand four hundred and fifty dollars. Its eight hundred and forty-nine thousand bitcoin were worth about sixty-three point six billion on Sunday, below the sixty-four point one billion it paid. Spot bitcoin E T Fs lost one point two eight billion dollars last week, their worst week since June, which turned twenty twenty-six flows negative. Strive, on the other hand, kept buying and added about fifteen thousand bitcoin in October. Corporate treasury clients sitting on paper losses will care about cost basis tracking, impairment reporting and board ready exports. Good time to check our lot level reporting against what their finance teams actually need.

And retirement money. A rule that would open four oh one k plans to alternative assets, crypto included, is now under White House review. CoinTelegraph notes that altcoins aren't excluded but face practical hurdles. If this gets finalized, record keepers and plan sponsors will need qualified custody with E R I S A grade reporting. A short discovery pass on what a record keeper integration would need could pay off before any requests for proposal show up.

Now regulation and policy.

We covered MEXC up top, so just the extra detail. Treasury says MEXC facilitated about sixteen billion dollars in illicit finance tied to Iran, North Korea and Russia. The order is part of what Treasury calls Operation Economic Outcast. For us, MEXC is a blocked counterparty for any client with a US nexus. We need a rule for direct versus indirect exposure, and an operations runbook for holding and returning inbound funds.

On the travel rule, the withdrawn proposal dates back to twenty twenty. Coin Center says it's the second surveillance proposal from that year to be pulled. Keep three thousand dollars as the US default, while still supporting stricter settings for E U clients, where the Transfer of Funds Regulation has no minimum.

Then the midterms. Democrats now control Congress, and the CLARITY Act, the market structure bill, faces a harder path. CoinTelegraph expects consumer protection and ethics demands in any deal, and Bankless sums up the next Congress as investigations, not legislation. Senator Blumenthal has already given Binance, Tether and Trump linked crypto firms until January fourth to hand over records, though as ranking member he can't issue subpoenas on his own. My read: don't plan roadmap items around CLARITY passing in twenty twenty-seven. The GENIUS Act and agency rules are what we can count on. And expect more oversight requests to land on counterparties and vendors.

Last in this section, O FAC sanctioned eight Canadian money service firms over alleged Iran links and listed six Tron addresses. More Tron U S D T addresses on the sanctions list means we should confirm our screening refreshes O FAC lists within hours and re screens existing whitelisted addresses, not just new transfers.

Final section: smart contracts and infrastructure.

OpenAI says an automated system found nine hundred and forty-eight Linux security flaws and produced working exploits and draft fixes, at about twelve hundred and seventy-nine dollars per flaw. Put that next to the t B T C kernel incident and the picture is clear. Finding exploitable bugs in the operating system under our signers is getting cheap. Patch speed and host hardening are product risks now, not just infrastructure hygiene. I'd raise this at the next security roadmap review.

On Ethereum, developers still won't fork the Hoodi testnet for the Glamsterdam upgrade until they resolve concerns about eight smart contract invariants affected by E I P eight oh three seven and E I P eight oh three eight. CoinDesk reports two rival proposals aimed at keeping older contracts working. Two weeks on, it's still unresolved. Safe style multisigs and any contract wallets with hard coded gas assumptions are exactly what's at risk, so we keep the testnet regression ticket open and track which proposal wins.

And a quick quantum note. Bankless describes faster deposits, cheaper contracts and a post quantum signature check all reaching Ethereum without a hard fork. On the Bitcoin side, developer Cory Fields proposed making quantum attackers post bitcoin as collateral before they can touch vulnerable coins, giving prepared owners a chance to recover funds and collect a penalty. Post quantum options are showing up at the contract level before the protocol level. That's useful input for the one page quantum readiness position we talked about last week.
